## Passage: The Disappearing Neighbourhood — Urbanisation and the Death of Traditional Communities

> In late **April** of last year, the city council approved a redevelopment plan that would forever alter the landscape of the old Riverside district. The neighbourhood, once a **beautiful** cluster of **brick** houses and narrow lanes running along the **bank** of the city's main river, had been home to generations of working-class families. Elderly residents would sit on their **balcony** overlooking the **beach** and the water, watching children play **baseball** in the streets while their parents chatted at the corner **barber** shop or picked up fresh **bacon** and **beef** from the local **butcher**. It was a community where everyone knew everyone — a place where a **boy** could ride his **bicycle** to the **cafeteria** after school, buy a **candy** bar with a few coins, and still feel safe.

> The plan, championed by a powerful real estate **broker**, proposed to replace the old neighbourhood with a complex of luxury high-rises, each unit capped with a glass **balcony** and a sleek **bathroom** fitted with modern **bulb**s and designer fixtures. The project would also introduce a private **aviation** club, an indoor **basketball** arena, a **bowling** alley, and a members-only **cafe** serving imported **beverage**s and artisanal **cake**s. Promotional **brochure**s depicted a **beautiful** future of glass and steel, promising prosperity. But **aside** from the glossy renderings, few tangible benefits were offered to the existing residents. Many felt **blue** about the impending demolition — it was not merely the loss of their homes but the erasure of an entire way of life.

> The resistance was swift. A local **athlete** — a former **baseball** champion turned community organiser — led the opposition. She stood before the crowd at a town hall meeting, gripping the lectern as if it were a **bat**, and declared that the council's plan was nothing more than an attempt to **butter** up developers **because** it served their financial interests. "Do not **buy** their promises," she warned. "These people want to push us **away** from the homes we built with our own hands." Her words resonated deeply. A retired **brother**hood of dockworkers, who had spent decades loading **cabbage** and **banana** shipments along the river, joined the protest. So did a young **astronaut**-turned-teacher who had returned to the neighbourhood to raise her family, and an amateur **astronomer** who hosted stargazing nights on the roof of the old **cabin** that served as the community's informal gathering place.

> The developers, undeterred, launched a public relations campaign. They hired a media firm to produce a glossy **bulletin** distributed to every household in the district, touting the economic benefits of the project. Housing prices, they claimed, would **balloon** once the new development was completed, attracting wealthy investors — a new class of **bull**s eager to snap up properties in the revitalised district. The city would impose a **cap** on affordable housing units, limiting them to a mere ten percent of the total, while the remainder would be sold at market rates. The **broom** of progress, they argued, needed to sweep away the old to make room for the new. Every **brick** of the old neighbourhood, every **bean** of the community garden, every **butterfly** that flitted through the **bush**es — all of it would be sacrificed **at** the altar of modernity.

> Yet the fight was never just about buildings. It was about the **care** with which communities are woven together over decades. It was about the elderly woman who sold **biscuit**s and **beer** from her front porch, the **barber** who had cut hair for three generations of the same family, the priest who carried a worn **bible** to Sunday services at the chapel by the **canal**. It was about the children who learned to swim **at** the **basin** of the river, the teenagers who played **basketball** until the streetlights flickered on, and the old men who argued over games of chess beside the **canteen**. These were the invisible threads that held the neighbourhood together — threads that no amount of money could replicate. A sociologist from the local university published a **blog** post arguing that when traditional communities are destroyed, the social fabric **cake**s into something brittle and fractured, losing the very elasticity that once gave it strength.

> The debate eventually drew national attention. A documentary filmmaker arrived with a **camera** to capture the story, interviewing residents young and old. She filmed a **butcher** explaining how he sourced **beef** and **bacon** from local farms, a grandmother teaching her grandson how to paddle a **canoe** along the river, and a young **boy** carrying a **briefcase** to his first job interview at a company across town — a **car** dealer that specialised in **automobile**s and **auto** parts. She captured the quiet dignity of a woman hanging freshly laundered **blouse**s and **brown** trousers on a clothesline behind her **brick** house, and the laughter of children sharing a **birthday** **cake** in the narrow alley between two buildings. The filmmaker noted, with evident **care**, that the neighbourhood represented something increasingly rare in modern cities: a place where people did not merely reside but genuinely belonged.

> In the end, the city council voted to proceed with the redevelopment, though a handful of concessions were extracted. A small community centre would be preserved, a few affordable housing units would be set **aside**, and a pedestrian walkway along the river **bank** would remain open to the public. But for most residents, these were hollow victories. The old neighbourhood — with its **black** iron gates, its **blackboard** walls where children once drew chalk murals, its modest **bed**s and breakfast inns, its **bell** that rang from the chapel tower every morning — would be reduced to memory. As one elderly resident remarked, her voice heavy with resignation: "They can build all the towers they want, but they cannot put back what they have taken **away**. This place was not just **brick** and **brown**stone. It was a **beautiful**, living thing — and now we must **ask** ourselves whether the price of progress was worth paying."

### 🔑 重点句子翻译练习

1. > "**Aside** from the glossy renderings, few tangible benefits were offered to the existing residents. Many felt **blue** about the impending demolition — it was not merely the loss of their homes but the erasure of an entire way of life."
>
> 翻译：除了那些光鲜亮丽的渲染图之外，几乎没有任何切实的利益提供给现有居民。许多人对即将到来的拆除感到忧郁——这不仅仅是失去家园，更是一种整个生活方式的消亡。

2. > "She stood before the crowd at a town hall meeting, gripping the lectern as if it were a **bat**, and declared that the council's plan was nothing more than an attempt to **butter** up developers **because** it served their financial interests."
>
> 翻译：她在市政厅会议上站在人群面前，紧紧抓住讲台，仿佛那是一根球棒，并宣称市议会的计划不过是试图巴结开发商，因为这符合他们的经济利益。

3. > "Housing prices, they claimed, would **balloon** once the new development was completed, attracting wealthy investors — a new class of **bulls** eager to snap up properties in the revitalised district."
>
> 翻译：他们声称，一旦新开发项目完成，房价将会猛增，吸引富有的投资者——一群急切想在复兴区域抢购房产的新的买空者阶层。

4. > "A sociologist from the local university published a **blog** post arguing that when traditional communities are destroyed, the social fabric **cake**s into something brittle and fractured, losing the very elasticity that once gave it strength."
>
> 翻译：当地大学的一位社会学家发表了一篇博客文章，认为当传统社区被摧毁时，社会结构会结成僵硬的硬块，变得脆弱和断裂，失去曾经赋予它力量的弹性。

5. > "The city would impose a **cap** on affordable housing units, limiting them to a mere ten percent of the total, while the remainder would be sold **at** market rates."
>
> 翻译：城市将对经济适用房设定最高限额，将其限制在仅占总数的百分之十，而其余部分将以市场价格出售。

6. > "It was about the **care** with which communities are woven together over decades."
>
> 翻译：这关乎的是几十年来社区是以怎样的细心编织在一起的。
